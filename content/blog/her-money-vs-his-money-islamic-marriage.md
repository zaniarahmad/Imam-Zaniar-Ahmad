# Her Money vs His Money: Who Owns What in an Islamic Marriage?

Money is one of those topics people avoid until it becomes a problem. Couples talk about where they'll live, how many kids they want, and which family they'll visit on Eid, but the money question gets pushed to the side because it feels awkward or unromantic.

The good news is that Islam doesn't leave us guessing here. It actually gives a very clear picture of who owns what, who is responsible for what, and how a couple can handle money together without anyone being treated unfairly. So let's go through it, step by step, in plain language.

## Two separate purses

Here's the foundation. In Islam, marriage does not merge property into one shared pot. A husband and a wife each keep a separate, independent financial identity. Scholars call this *dhimmah maliyyah mustaqillah*.

In other words, marriage joins two lives, not two bank accounts.

That might sound cold at first, but it's actually a protection for both of them. Each person's rights are clear from the beginning. Knowing this from day one prevents a lot of injustice later.

## Her wealth is hers alone

What she earns, what she is gifted, what she inherits, and what she receives as mahr all belong to her. Her husband has no ownership or control over it. She can buy, sell, invest, or give it away without needing his permission.

Allah says:

> وَآتُوا النِّسَاءَ صَدُقَاتِهِنَّ نِحْلَةً فَإِنْ طِبْنَ لَكُمْ عَنْ شَيْءٍ مِنْهُ نَفْسًا فَكُلُوهُ هَنِيئًا مَرِيئًا
>
> "And give the women [upon marriage] their bridal gifts graciously. But if they give up willingly to you anything of it, then take it in satisfaction and ease." (An-Nisa 4:4)

Notice the word *willingly*. Only what she gives from her own heart is his to enjoy.

### Advise, don't control

That doesn't mean he has to stay silent. A husband can advise his wife on being smart with her money, the same way any caring person would. But that's advice only, not control. He can share an opinion. He can't make the decision for her. The final say stays with her.

## His wealth, with a duty

His money is his own too. But unlike hers, it comes with a responsibility attached.

He is obligated to provide maintenance (*nafaqah*) for his wife and household. That duty stays on him even if his wife is wealthy. Her wealth does not cancel his obligation.

So the picture looks like this: her wealth is hers, and his wealth carries a duty.

## Does she have to work?

No. Providing is his obligation, not hers. A woman is not required in Islam to work or earn money to support herself, her husband, or her children.

And he cannot force her to work or take her salary. That is *dhulm*, injustice. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> أَلَا لَا تَظْلِمُوا، أَلَا لَا تَظْلِمُوا، إِنَّهُ لَا يَحِلُّ مَالُ امْرِئٍ إِلَّا بِطِيبِ نَفْسٍ مِنْهُ
>
> "Beware, do not commit injustice! Beware, do not commit injustice! Truly, the property of a Muslim is not lawful [for another] except by their own good will."

If she chooses to work, the two of them can agree on terms beforehand, for example around expenses that come directly from her working outside. But he still cannot take her money without her free consent.

And if she wants to help with the household, that's beautiful. It just has to come from goodwill (*tib nafs*), never from pressure or coercion.

## Two incomes? Agree first

Society is different now. Most of the time, both spouses are working, and that is an acceptable arrangement.

But it should be agreed upon beforehand. The default is that he is responsible for the necessities, and she does not need to work to maintain a certain lifestyle.

So if you're planning a life with two incomes, don't just assume it. Talk about it openly and agree on it before the nikkah, not after. That way nobody feels trapped into an arrangement they never actually chose.

## What he must cover

By custom (*ma'ruf*) and according to his means, he covers the essentials: food, clothing, housing, hygiene necessities, and medical care. Allah says:

> لِيُنفِقْ ذُو سَعَةٍ مِّن سَعَتِهِ ۖ وَمَن قُدِرَ عَلَيْهِ رِزْقُهُ فَلْيُنفِقْ مِمَّا آتَاهُ اللَّهُ
>
> "Let a man of wealth spend from his wealth, and he whose provision is restricted, let him spend from what Allah has given him." (At-Talaq 65:7)

Notice that this ayah ties provision to his capacity. A man with more spends from his abundance. A man with less spends from what Allah has given him.

## Fitting, not lavish

In his Farewell Sermon, the Prophet ﷺ said:

> فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ فِي النِّسَاءِ... وَلَهُنَّ عَلَيْكُمْ رِزْقُهُنَّ وَكِسْوَتُهُنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ
>
> "Fear Allah concerning women... and their right upon you is that you provide them with food and clothing in a fitting manner." (Sahih Muslim)

Now here's something I really want to stress. That list of food, clothing, housing, hygiene, and medical care is not exhaustive. It's there to show that the essentials must be taken care of.

But it should never be used to squeeze every dollar out of him, or to force luxuries he simply cannot afford. The standard is *fitting*, not *lavish*. Provision is a duty, and it's also a mercy. It shouldn't be turned into a weapon.

## Joint money, clear shares

Spouses can absolutely buy a home, a car, or a business together. Islam has no problem with couples building something side by side.

In that case, ownership is shared in proportion to what each person put in, unless they agree otherwise. And scholars strongly encourage documenting each share, to protect everyone's rights in case of divorce or death.

Write it down. It is not distrust, it is justice.

## Paper is not character

Many people sign premarital contracts, and that can be wise. But you cannot make a good agreement with a bad person.

No matter how detailed your contract is, you can only enforce it so far. In the end, it comes down to their character.

So look at the person before the paperwork. A contract can support a good marriage, but it can't create one.

## Talk money early

Conversations about money belong in the very early stages of discussing marriage with each other.

Not just in the last stages right before the nikkah, when feelings are invested and families are already planning. Talk about work, spending, provision, and expectations while you can both still choose freely.

Early honesty saves a lot of heartbreak.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does my husband own my salary or my mahr?**
No. What she earns, is gifted, inherits, or receives as mahr belongs to her alone. He has no ownership or control over it.

**If the wife is wealthy, does the husband still have to provide?**
Yes. His duty of maintenance (nafaqah) stays on him even if his wife is wealthy. Her wealth does not cancel his obligation.

**Is it okay for both spouses to work?**
Yes, it's an acceptable arrangement, but it should be agreed upon beforehand. The default is that he covers the necessities and she does not need to work to maintain a certain lifestyle. He cannot force her to work or take her salary.

**Do we need to document joint purchases?**
Scholars strongly encourage it. Ownership is shared in proportion to what each person contributed unless you agree otherwise, and writing each share down protects everyone's rights in case of divorce or death.

## The takeaway

Her wealth is hers. His wealth carries the duty to provide. Anything beyond that is given out of love and goodwill, agreed on early, written down clearly, and built on good character.
